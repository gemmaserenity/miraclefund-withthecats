The emergency we face

We have been told to leave our home by August 31.

We are Gemma and Sascha. On August 3, we received a written demand stating that our home will be placed on the market and that we must be fully out—with all of our cats—by August 31, 2026, at 11:59 p.m. Arizona time.

The message says no extension will be granted. It says the sale will move forward while we are still living here unless a fully funded purchase agreement is produced immediately. We are seeking appropriate legal review of our rights, including the effect of our cohabitation agreement. But legal questions do not stop the clock, feed the cats, or give our family a safe place to go.

Our goal is $500,000: enough to make a cash offer on a permanent Arizona home, move every cat safely, and give our family twelve months of stability while our businesses grow into sustainable income.

Why this is a medical and family emergency

The cats have been at the center of our lives for as long as either of us can remember. We grew up with them, found comfort in them, cared for them, and mourned them. To us, they have never been possessions. They are our children: distinct little lives with whom we share years of love, trust, routine, and belonging.

For Sascha, that bond carries another dimension. He lives with myelomeningocele, unshunted hydrocephalus, Arnold–Chiari II malformation, and severe neurogenic pain that never switches off. The cats' warmth, purring, gentle pressure, familiar presence, and daily routines help him regulate stress, redirect attention from pain, sleep, and remain engaged when a flare makes ordinary activity difficult. Each cat knows his body, his boundaries, and his quiet signs of distress.

How we arrived here

We came to the United States to rebuild a life after years marked by domestic violence and upheaval. Sascha—an American citizen who knew he would never have biological children because of his disability—made that move for Gemma and her children. When Gemma’s adult son needed a safe place and intensive support, we opened our home and reorganized our lives around helping him build a future.

For nearly ten years, we devoted some of our most active working years to helping my son through recurring mental-health crises and profound periods of instability. We helped him preserve employment, keep a business running, regain his footing after setbacks, and make it through moments when ordinary life became overwhelming. That constant scaffolding came at a real cost: our own income, businesses, savings, and long-term security repeatedly had to come second.

When we bought our home in Maricopa in 2023, my son’s conventional employment helped the household qualify for the mortgage. At the same time, we entered into a cohabitation agreement intended to document our equal stake in the home we had all worked to create and preserve.

Then, at 2:34 a.m. on March 7, 2026, our family structure changed overnight. My son left without warning. The household and shared-business income we had relied upon stopped, and the financial support promised in the letter he left behind did not materialize. We suddenly had to sustain ourselves, the mortgage, utilities, and every cat without predictable revenue.

We did not stop. We rebuilt our work to operate virtually, learned new technologies, and created the foundations of new digital businesses. We asked for enough time to bring the mortgage current, strengthen our finances, and pursue a properly funded transfer of the home into our own names. Instead, my son—the person named on the title—has stated in writing that he intends to sell and that we must leave before our new work has had time to carry us.

That is the heartbreak at the center of this campaign: after a decade spent helping my son remain housed, employed, and able to move forward, his decision now places our own home and stability at risk. A rushed sale in a difficult market could erase the home we fought so hard to keep, while more affordable distressed properties compete in the same area. We cannot build our future around another person’s decision ever again.

Why an ordinary rental is not a real solution

Most rentals restrict the number of animals allowed. Splitting up the cats would fracture a bonded family and remove the continuous circle of companionship that helps Sascha through unpredictable pain and bedbound periods. For us, securing a home we can control is not a luxury—it is the only durable way to protect the whole family and prevent this crisis from repeating.

A cash purchase gives us what another fragile arrangement cannot: speed, certainty, no mortgage-qualification delay while our businesses are still scaling, and control over a home where every cat is welcome. Your support will cover the purchase, a safe move, immediate living and cat-care costs, and one year to turn the work we have built into dependable revenue.

Once our family is secure, we dream of exploring a properly organized nonprofit cat sanctuary: a lasting refuge where vulnerable cats can be safe, loved, and cared for. That is a future aspiration, not a promise or an existing charity. This campaign remains a personal fundraiser, and gifts are not represented as tax-deductible.

Any amount moves us closer to safety. If you cannot give, sharing this page with someone who understands that animals can be family—and vital disability support—is an equally meaningful act.

More than companionship

Why the cats are essential to Sascha’s daily life

Sascha’s neurological conditions can bring relentless burning, electric, and pressure-related pain, disrupted sleep, limited mobility, and deep fatigue. The cats cannot cure those conditions. What they provide is continuous, familiar support that makes the symptoms more bearable.

  • Calming sensory input: purring, warmth, touch, and familiar weight can help redirect attention from pain and ease stress.
  • Support through flare-ups: different cats stay near him at different times, providing companionship without exhausting a single animal.
  • Routine and purpose: feeding, care, and interaction create structure even on days dominated by pain.
  • A bond that cannot be replaced: these cats have learned Sascha’s movements, sensitive areas, and subtle signs that he is struggling.
